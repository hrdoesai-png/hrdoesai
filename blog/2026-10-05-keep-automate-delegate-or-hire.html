Keep, Automate, Delegate, or Hire: Where Your Next HR Dollar Should Go

By Amy Hagan, Founder of HR Does AI

Every growing company hits the same moment. The HR inbox is overflowing, managers keep asking the same questions, and the person who "handles HR" (usually on top of three other jobs) is running on fumes.

The instinct is to open a requisition. Sometimes that's right. But a job description written in a hurry usually describes the pain, not the need.

There's a better first move: sort the work.

Four buckets, one decision

Take every recurring HR task, the real ones, not the org chart version, and put each one in a bucket.

Keep. Work that needs experienced human judgment and stays with your current people. Coaching a manager through a hard conversation. Deciding how to respond to a harassment complaint. Weighing an accommodation request. These are the moments where trust is built or lost.

Automate. Work that follows the same steps every time and doesn't need a judgment call. Reminders, routing, acknowledgment tracking, new-hire document collection, compliance calendar alerts. If someone is running a checklist from memory, it's a candidate.

Delegate. Work that matters but doesn't need your most experienced person. Some of it belongs with managers, once they have clear guidance. Some belongs with payroll or benefits partners you already pay. Some belongs with an administrative role rather than a senior one.

Hire. Work that is important, recurring, judgment-heavy, and not getting done well by anyone right now. That's the gap a new hire should fill, and now you can describe it precisely.

What the sorting usually reveals

When leaders do this honestly, a few patterns show up.

The Keep bucket is smaller than people expect, and more important. Much of what feels like HR overload is administrative volume piled on top of a handful of high-stakes responsibilities.

A lot of the Automate work is manual because no one ever designed the process. Often the company already owns a system that could handle it.

The Delegate bucket exposes a manager problem. When managers don't know how to handle routine questions, everything escalates to HR. Better manager guidance can relieve more pressure than another employee would.

And the Hire bucket gets sharper. Instead of "we need an HR person," you can say "we need someone who can handle employee relations and coach managers through performance issues." That's a better job posting, and a better hire.

Where AI fits

AI shows up in two of the four buckets, and it's worth being specific about how.

In Automate, AI and workflow tools can handle routing, reminders, document collection, and first drafts of standard communications.

In Keep, AI can support the person doing the work, but it doesn't take over. It can organize notes, build a timeline, or draft a summary for review. The human still owns the decision and every word that goes out.

What AI should not do is make the call in a sensitive situation or replace the relationships a good HR professional builds. If your plan has a tool deciding who gets disciplined or whether a complaint is credible, the plan needs work.

Start this week

You don't need outside help to begin. Try this:

  • Ask whoever handles HR to list every recurring task from the last month.
  • Next to each one, note how often it happens and roughly how long it takes.
  • Mark which tasks require judgment and which follow the same steps every time.
  • Circle anything only one person knows how to do.

Even a rough list shows you where the time goes. It may also tell you the next hire is a different role than you assumed.

Decide with facts

You may still decide to hire, and that may be exactly right. The difference is you'll be hiring for a defined need, not a general feeling of being overwhelmed.
