Before You Hire Another HR Person, Audit the Work

Amy Hagan, Founder of HR Does AI

Your company is growing. Employees have questions. Managers need help. Onboarding is inconsistent, someone is tracking leave on a spreadsheet, and important HR tasks live inside three inboxes and one person's memory.

So someone says, "I think we need to hire another HR person."

Maybe you do. But a new hire costs far more than the salary on the offer letter. Once you add benefits, payroll taxes, software licenses, equipment, and recruiting, the total is commonly estimated at 1.25 to 1.4 times base pay, every year.

Before you commit to that, ask a different question: Do you need another person, or do you need better systems?

Those are not the same problem.

More work does not always require more people

For years, companies have treated growing administrative work as an automatic signal to add staff. Onboarding takes too long? Hire someone. Questions piling up? Hire someone. Managers can't find anything? Hire someone whose new job will apparently be finding things.

That may relieve the immediate pressure. It can also build an expensive department around inefficient processes. A growing workload should trigger an evaluation before it triggers a job posting.

Start by looking at the actual work:

  • Which tasks are repetitive?
  • Which activities require professional judgment?
  • Where is information entered more than once?
  • What are employees asking over and over?
  • Which processes depend too heavily on one person?
  • Where are the delays?
  • What could be automated, standardized, or made self-service?
  • Where is human involvement essential?

The answers lead to a much better staffing decision. You may genuinely need an experienced HR professional. An administrative role may be enough. Outside strategic support may fit better than another employee. Or much of the pressure may ease through better workflows and smarter use of technology you already own.

This is not about avoiding HR investment. It is about making the right one.

AI should support judgment, not replace it

Let's address the nervous robot in the room.

Using AI in HR does not mean handing employment decisions to a machine and hoping for the best. Technology should not decide who is hired, believed, disciplined, promoted, accommodated, or terminated. Those calls require experience, context, empathy, legal awareness, and human judgment.

Workplace investigations show where the line belongs. AI can help schedule interviews, organize evidence, build timelines, and keep documentation consistent. It should never assess a witness's credibility or decide whether an allegation is substantiated. That work belongs to a trained investigator who can be held accountable for the conclusion.

But HR professionals also spend enormous time on work that doesn't need a blank page every time. AI can draft, organize, summarize policies, structure routine communications, build checklists, prepare training materials, and make internal information easier to find.

Used responsibly, AI doesn't remove the human side of HR. It frees up time for it. Your HR leader should be coaching managers, addressing risk, and planning for growth, not reformatting the same document for the fourteenth time.

Automation is not the same as "using ChatGPT"

Asking an AI tool to write an occasional email is not the same as redesigning an HR process. Real HR automation requires understanding:

  • The purpose of the process and the employment risks involved
  • Which decisions require human review
  • What employees and managers actually need
  • The systems the company already uses
  • What data should never go into an unapproved tool
  • The checkpoints that prevent errors
  • Who remains accountable for the outcome

If a seven-step process contains four unnecessary steps, automating all seven just lets you do unnecessary work faster. Congratulations, I suppose, but that is not transformation. Fix the process first. Then decide what technology should support it.

What an HR AI audit uncovers

An HR AI audit maps how HR work moves through your organization and where time, money, and attention are lost. It typically covers five areas:

  • Hiring and onboarding: recruiting, candidate communication, new-hire paperwork
  • Employee and manager support: policy questions, manager guidance, training
  • Leave and compliance: leave and accommodation administration, compliance calendars, recurring notices
  • Employee relations: intake, performance documentation, investigation administration
  • Reporting and transitions: workforce reporting, planning, offboarding, knowledge transfer

Every task then lands in one of three categories:

  • Work that can be automated or streamlined
  • Work AI can support, with human review
  • Work that stays firmly in human hands

That distinction matters. The best answer is rarely "automate everything." It is to automate deliberately and protect the decisions that require professional judgment.

Why I approach it this way

I've spent nearly 30 years in HR across government, healthcare, manufacturing, supply chain, and education, including senior roles at PepsiCo, Goodyear, and Sysco. As a CHRO, I helped scale a behavioral health organization from about 200 employees to more than 800. I've also conducted more than 150 workplace investigations and hold the SPHR and AWI-CH credentials.

That experience shapes every recommendation. I know which HR work can safely move faster, and which work carries too much risk to hand to a tool.

Before you approve the position

You may still decide to hire. If you do, you'll know exactly what expertise you need and why. Or you may find the real answer is a better process, a smarter system, targeted consulting support, or a mix of all three.

Headcount is expensive. Confusion is expensive too. A thoughtful HR AI audit helps you stop paying for both.
